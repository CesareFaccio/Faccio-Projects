How the Wind Tunnel Works

How it works

Draw any shape, and the Wind Tunnel blows air past it and lets it turn until it finds an angle it is happy sitting at. The air is not an animation: it is a fluid simulation, solving the real equations for how air moves, recomputed from scratch sixty times a second on your own graphics card. This page explains what it is doing, why the result is often the opposite of what people expect, and where the whole thing stops being trustworthy.

What you are looking at

Air enters along the bottom of the tunnel and leaves at the top. The dotted texture drifting upwards is smoke, injected at the bottom edge purely so you can see the flow; it has no weight and does not push on anything. It is a marker, not a participant.

Your shape sits in the middle, pinned at the small cross. It cannot drift, and it cannot be blown downstream. It can only rotate about that one point. That restriction is deliberate: a shape free to move as well as turn would simply be swept out of the tunnel in a second, and the interesting question is not whether air pushes things along, which everyone already knows, but which way round a shape chooses to sit.

The second window plots the twisting force, the torque, against the angle the shape is currently at. Where that curve crosses zero, the air is not twisting the shape at all, which means the shape can rest there. Those crossings are the equilibrium angles, and they are the actual answer to the question the tunnel is asking.

Using it

  • Drag inside the tunnel to draw. Whatever you draw is closed into a solid automatically; you do not have to join the ends up yourself.
  • Teardrop and Plate load two shapes worth knowing: a classic streamlined section, and a flat plate, which is the simplest shape that shows the behaviour described below.
  • Reset air clears the flow and starts it again without touching your shape. Useful after a big slider change, when the old flow pattern is still working itself out.
  • Pause freezes everything, which is the only way to actually look at a wake rather than watch it go past.

Drawing something much bigger than the tunnel scales it down to fit rather than rejecting it. A shape reaching the ends of the tunnel is fighting the inflow and outflow conditions rather than the air, and the results stop meaning anything.

The result that surprises people

Pin a shape at its centre, and it settles across the flow, broadside on, presenting the largest possible area to the air. This is almost always the opposite of what people predict, because it seems to contradict the intuition that things line up with the wind.

But it is correct, and you have seen it many times. Drop a playing card and it lands flat, fluttering, rather than knifing down edge first. Drop a leaf and the same thing happens. Neither one turns itself into an arrow.

The reason is where the air's push effectively acts. When a shape sits at a slight angle to the flow, the push does not act at the middle of the shape; it acts somewhere ahead of the middle, towards the upstream end. Call that the centre of pressure. If the shape is pinned behind that point, which is what happens when you pin it at its centre, then any small tilt produces a twist that increases the tilt. It runs away, and it keeps running away until the shape is fully broadside and the situation is symmetric again. Broadside is where it stops.

Now move the Balance point slider towards the nose. You are pinning the shape ahead of the centre of pressure, and the same arithmetic reverses: a small tilt now produces a twist that reduces the tilt. It self corrects, and the shape lines up along the flow.

That is precisely how a dart, an arrow and a weathervane work. None of them is shaped to point into the wind by magic; all of them are held well ahead of where the air pushes, whether by a heavy tip, a mounted pivot, or the fins pushing the effective point of action backwards. Move the pivot and you change the answer, not just the strength of it. The slider is showing you the whole of aerodynamic stability in one control.

How the air is simulated

The tunnel is divided into a grid of small square cells, and each cell holds one number for how fast the air in it is moving and in what direction. Every frame, the whole grid is updated in four stages. This is the standard structure of a fluid solver, and it is worth knowing because each stage corresponds to something physical.

  • Push air in at the bottom. The cells along the inlet are simply told what their velocity is, and smoke is injected there at the same time.
  • Carry everything along with the flow. Air carries its own motion with it, and it carries the smoke too. This stage is called advection and it is described in more detail below, because it turned out to matter enormously.
  • Let momentum spread sideways. Fast moving air drags slower air next to it along. That is viscosity, and it is what the Turbulence slider controls.
  • Make it add up. Air cannot pile up in one place or vanish from another. After the previous stages it generally does not obey that, so the solver works out a pressure field whose push exactly cancels the piling up, and subtracts it. This is the expensive stage: it takes forty passes over the whole grid, and it is where most of the frame's work goes.

That last stage is also where pressure comes from. It is not an extra ingredient that gets added; it is whatever it has to be to stop air accumulating. Every force the shape feels is a consequence of it.

Why advection needed fixing

The simple way to carry air along is to look backwards: for each cell, work out where the air currently in it must have come from a moment ago, and go and fetch the value from there. That works, never explodes, and is what most browser fluid demos do.

It also blurs, every single step. The place you look back to almost never lands exactly on a cell, so the value gets mixed from the neighbouring cells, and mixing is smoothing. Sixty times a second, that smoothing behaves exactly like a thick, syrupy viscosity that nobody asked for. It was large enough to swamp anything the Turbulence slider did, which is why an early version of this tunnel could not produce a proper turbulent wake at any setting: it was being held smooth by its own arithmetic.

The fix is to measure the error rather than try to avoid it. Carry the field forwards one step, then carry the result backwards one step. With perfect arithmetic you would land exactly where you started, so however far off you end up is the error, and half of it can be subtracted back out. The result is clamped so the correction can never invent a value that existed nowhere nearby, which is what keeps it stable. It costs two extra passes over the grid, and it is the single change that made the wake real.

The shape in the flow

The shape is not a hole cut in the grid. The grid covers the whole tunnel, including the inside of the shape, and the solver simply insists that the air inside the shape moves exactly as the solid does. Since the shape can rotate, that means the air inside it is driven to match a rigid body spinning at the shape's current rate, rather than being driven to a standstill.

That distinction sounds pedantic and is not. Forcing the interior to stand still would mean the surface never resists the shape's own rotation, and with light damping the shape would spin up and keep spinning forever, driven by nothing. Matching the rotation instead is what makes a spinning shape feel the air fight it, which is exactly right: spinning something in air is harder than spinning it in a vacuum.

Measuring the twist

The force on the shape comes from the pressure pushing inwards all over its surface. Adding that up around the outline, weighted by distance from the pivot, gives the torque, and that number is what drives the rotation.

You may notice that the tunnel reports torque and never reports drag. That is not an oversight; drag turned out to be genuinely unmeasurable here, and the reason is instructive. Driving air through a tunnel requires a pressure difference from one end to the other, and that background gradient pushes on everything in the tunnel, including the shape. Its contribution to the force is not small and is very hard to separate from the force the shape's own geometry produces. Three different approaches to subtracting it all failed the same sanity check: a circular disc, which by symmetry must produce zero of everything interesting, kept reporting a drag that varied with angle.

Torque does not have this problem, and the reason is neat. A uniform push across the whole shape acts, in effect, at its centre, so it produces no twist about that point at all. The background gradient drops out of the torque completely while corrupting the drag badly. So the tunnel reports the quantity it can actually measure honestly, and stays quiet about the one it cannot. The same disc test now returns essentially zero torque at every angle, which is the correct answer.

The sliders

Wind speed

How fast air enters the tunnel. It has a firm upper limit, and the limit is set by arithmetic rather than by taste. The solver moves air one step at a time, and if the air crosses more than about two cells in a single step, the scheme stops resolving the flow and starts smearing it, and the pressure field goes with it. Past that point the numbers are confidently wrong, which is worse than a shorter slider. At the top of the range the simulation quietly splits each frame into several smaller steps to stay inside that limit.

Turbulence

This is the air's thickness, its viscosity, running from syrup on the left to something close to thin air on the right. What actually changes is the Reynolds number, the ratio between how hard the flow is being pushed and how strongly the air resists being sheared.

At the thick end, the flow stays glued to the surface of the shape and the wake closes quietly behind it. As the air thins, the flow can no longer follow the shape's curve all the way round, separates from the surface, and the wake broadens. Thinner still and the wake stops being steady: vortices peel off alternate sides in a regular rhythm, each one giving the shape a small kick as it goes. That is what buffets the shape instead of letting it sit still, and it is the same mechanism that makes a flag flap and a cable hum in the wind.

The slider cannot reach zero viscosity, and this is a real limit rather than a missing feature. A grid of finite cells cannot represent detail smaller than one cell. With nothing to smooth out the small scales, the flow keeps rolling vorticity up into ever finer structures until they are finer than the cells, at which point the simulation is no longer computing anything meaningful and the local speeds run away. So the tunnel enforces a floor: the viscosity is never allowed to drop below what the current grid can actually carry at the current wind speed. That is also why a faster machine sees more turbulence, as explained next, rather than just a sharper picture.

Rotational inertia

How reluctant the shape is to change how fast it is spinning. Heavy on the right: it responds slowly, overshoots its resting angle and swings past it before coming back. Light on the left: it snaps to the answer almost immediately, which is useful when you just want to know where a shape settles rather than watch it get there.

Angular damping

How much the rotation is bled away, as a stand in for pivot friction. Low damping with high inertia gives a shape that oscillates about its resting angle for a long time, which is worth seeing at least once: the oscillation is the shape genuinely being restored towards equilibrium and repeatedly overshooting it. High damping settles quickly and quietly.

Balance point

Where along the shape's own long axis the pivot sits. This is the control that changes the answer rather than the journey, for the reasons in the section above. Centre for broadside, towards the nose for weathervaning.

It adapts to your device

Resolution is the one thing that genuinely buys turbulence, because the smoothing baked into the arithmetic shrinks as the cells do, so a finer grid can honestly reach a higher Reynolds number. It is also expensive: the cell count goes up with the square, and a finer grid needs shorter time steps as well, so the top setting is roughly five times the work of the bottom one.

So when the page opens, it briefly measures how fast your graphics card actually shades pixels by running a small burst of work and timing it, then picks the finest grid that should still fit inside a frame's budget. A laptop with integrated graphics gets the coarse grid and a smooth sixty frames a second; a desktop card or an Apple Silicon machine gets the fine one, and a visibly more detailed wake.

Importantly, the physics does not change with the grid. Every quantity in the solver is expressed in terms of the tunnel itself rather than in cells, and converted at the point of use, so a wind speed of 0.7 means the same flow whether it is being resolved on 180 cells or 320. A shape settles at the same angle on both; the faster machine simply sees more of the fine structure on the way there.

The trace window

The plot records the twist the air is applying against the angle the shape is at, building up as the shape moves. The dashed line is zero torque.

Every crossing of that line is an angle where the air is not twisting the shape, so the shape could rest there. But only half of them are places it will actually stay. Where the curve crosses from positive to negative, a small nudge produces a twist pushing back towards the crossing, so the shape returns: that is a stable equilibrium. Where it crosses the other way, a small nudge produces a twist pushing further away, so the shape leaves: unstable. A shape pinned at its centre typically has stable crossings at broadside and unstable ones in line with the flow, and moving the balance point towards the nose swaps them over. You can watch the swap happen on the plot as you drag the slider.

What it is not

  • It is two dimensional. Real wakes are three dimensional, and real turbulence has a way of stretching vortices that simply does not exist in a flat world. Two dimensional turbulence behaves qualitatively differently from the real thing, tending to pool energy into large structures rather than cascading it down to small ones.
  • The numbers are not in real units. There are no newtons and no metres per second here. Everything is measured in tunnel widths and simulation seconds. Ratios and comparisons between shapes mean something; absolute values do not.
  • The air cannot be compressed and there is no heat and no sound. That is a good assumption for anything well below the speed of sound and a useless one above it.
  • The Reynolds numbers are far below reality. A real wing operates in air thousands of times thinner, relatively speaking, than anything this grid can resolve. What you are seeing is the right mechanism at the wrong scale.
  • It is not a design tool. It will tell you why a dart has its weight at the front, and it will not tell you anything reliable about a wing section you are actually planning to build.
Worth trying: load the Plate, leave the balance point at the centre, and watch it turn broadside. Then drag the balance point towards the nose without changing anything else. The same shape in the same air picks the opposite answer, which is the single most useful thing in this simulation.
